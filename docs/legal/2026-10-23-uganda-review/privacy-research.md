## Findings that should drive the policy and controls

### Scope, roles, and the records in this product

* **Act ss. 1–2:** the Act applies to processing in Uganda and to a person outside Uganda processing personal data relating to Ugandan citizens. “Personal data” includes information from which a person can be identified, including identity data; “processing” includes collection, use, disclosure, alignment, erasure, and destruction. A controller determines the purposes and manner; a processor processes on behalf of a controller.
* Akolet/CampusHomes is likely a controller for its own accounts, listings, tenant/landlord records, product events, admin operations, and AI feature purposes (application of the statutory definitions to the product is an inference). A vendor is a processor only to the extent it processes on Akolet’s instructions; if it determines its own purposes or means, treat it as a separate controller until counsel confirms otherwise.
* The current code review supplied for this research shows Groq receives free-user text plus 12-message history for an AI feature, and product-event search functions store free-text `q` values. The policy statement that “search text is never stored” is therefore too broad unless the event path is changed or the statement is narrowed. Browser page-path analytics are a different record from search query text.

### Principles, purpose, and notice

* **Act s. 3(1)(a)–(g):** every collector, processor, and controller must be accountable; collect/process fairly and lawfully; keep data adequate, relevant, and not excessive; retain only for the period authorised by law or required; keep quality; ensure transparency and participation; and use security safeguards.
* **Act s. 12:** collection must have a lawful, specific, explicitly defined purpose related to the collector/controller’s functions.
* **Act s. 13(1):** before collection, tell the data subject the nature/category of data, responsible person’s name/address, purpose, whether supply is discretionary/mandatory, consequences of not supplying it, legal/authorised requirement, recipients, access/rectification rights, and retention period. The privacy notice should name the controller, give a DPO/contact route, list Groq or other recipients where applicable, explain transfer/hosting, and give a retention period or rule for each meaningful category.
* The tenant agreement reportedly includes optional medical conditions/allergies and optional national ID/passport data. Health status/medical records are “special personal data” under **Act s. 9(1)**; ID/passport data is personal data under **s. 2**. Do not hide these in one undifferentiated broad consent: identify the fields and purposes, make optional health fields genuinely optional, and obtain a freely given, specific consent where s. 9(3)(b) is relied on.
* **Section 9(1) also expressly covers financial information**, religious/philosophical beliefs, political opinion and sexual life. Rent/payment confirmations can therefore require special-data analysis even without platform custody of funds. Section 9(3) permits the specified employer-duty, freely given information with consent, and qualifying non-profit-body conditions. A section 7 general contract, medical-purpose or legal-obligation basis must not be assumed to override section 9. Counsel must resolve the appropriate condition for necessary financial records and any emergency medical use before implementation.

### Consent and other statutory exceptions

* **Act s. 7(1):** prior data-subject consent is the default. The Act’s definition in **s. 2** requires consent to be freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous, and shown by a statement or clear affirmative action.
* **Act s. 7(2)(a)–(e):** processing may occur without that prior consent where authorised/required by law; necessary for a public body’s public duty, national security, or crime/law enforcement; necessary for a contract or pre-contract steps requested by the subject; for medical purposes; or for a legal obligation of the controller.
* **Reg. 10(1)–(5):** a subject’s objection is notified to the collector/processor/controller in Form 1. The objection rule does not apply to **s. 7(2)** processing or data subject to a controller/processor/collector’s “legitimate interest.” Regulation 10(3) defines that interest as processing the subject would reasonably expect or for a compelling justification, including fraud prevention, network/information security, crime/public-security threats, and internal administration; the burden is on the organisation (**Reg. 10(4)**).
* Drafting caution: Uganda’s Act does not use GDPR’s six-basis framework. Regulation 10’s “legitimate interest” is expressly an objection exception; do not present it as an unqualified general lawful basis without legal review. Where possible, map each CampusHomes purpose to s. 7(2), consent, or a clearly documented Reg. 10 interest and record the reasoning.

### Children

* **Act s. 8(a)–(c):** a person must not collect/process a child’s personal data unless there is prior consent from a parent/guardian or other authorised decision-maker, the processing is necessary to comply with law, or it is for research/statistical purposes.
* **Reg. 11:** each collector/processor/controller must establish an age-ascertainment system and, where data relates to a child, a way to obtain parent/legal-guardian consent where necessary.
* The privacy Act and Regulations reviewed do not themselves give the child age threshold. Read them with Uganda’s Constitution and Children Act and the Contracts Act capacity provisions. The parent review located the Children Act source trail and ordinary age-18 capacity rule, with exceptions. The proposed 18-plus ordinary CampusHomes account rule is a service policy; do not present it as a universal statutory ban on all contracts below that age. Use an age-assurance/guardian workflow for child data and confirm the lawful basis with counsel.

### Registration and DPO

* **Act s. 6:** for an institution, its head must designate a data protection officer responsible for compliance. The organisation should appoint and publish a DPO/contact route; whether a particular Akolet legal entity is an “institution” is an application question, but the registration form expects DPO details.
* **Act s. 29(1)–(3):** the Authority keeps the Data Protection Register and registers every person, institution, or public body collecting or processing personal data, with the application made in the prescribed manner.
* **Regs. 13–14:** the register may be electronic/manual and must be kept current; it records name, address, nature of personal data, and purpose.
* **Reg. 15(1)–(4):** every collector, processor, and controller must register, subject to exemptions later gazetted by the Office in consultation with the Board. Contravention is an offence punishable by up to six currency points or three months’ imprisonment (or both); corporations and responsible officers are covered by subreg. (4). No current Gazette exemption was located in this review, so confirm directly with PDPO before relying on one.
* **Reg. 16(2)–(3):** the application records roles, data categories, purposes, retention duration, recipients, DPO details, transfer countries, and security measures, and includes a written undertaking about processing/storing data outside Uganda. **Reg. 20** makes registration valid for 12 months; **Reg. 23(2)** requires renewal at least three months before expiry; **Reg. 27** requires changes to registered particulars within 14 days; **Reg. 28** makes register inspection free at reasonable times.

### Retention and deletion

* **Act s. 18(1):** do not retain longer than necessary for the collection/processing purpose unless retention is required/authorised by law, necessary for a lawful related function, required by contract, or consented to by the subject.
* **Act s. 18(2):** the ordinary limit does not apply to retention for crime/law enforcement, national security, pecuniary-penalty enforcement, public-revenue enforcement, proceedings, or historical/statistical/research purposes. **s. 18(3)** requires data used for decisions to be retained for the legal period or, if none, long enough to let the subject request access.
* **Act s. 18(4)–(5):** at the end of the period, destroy/delete or de-identify; destruction/deletion must prevent reconstruction in intelligible form. **Reg. 26** preserves security and Act compliance after a person ceases collecting/processing.
* The code review supplied for this task found a hard-delete/purge path (`deleteUser`/`purgeUser`/`runPurgeCascade`) but Logto deletion is best-effort and not awaited, and no object-storage purge was shown. Do not promise “deleted” or “purged” until those paths are verified end-to-end. Define separate schedules for account identity, IDs, health data, agreements, AI prompts/history, event logs, backups, and vendor copies. The reviewed search-event inserts do not supply actor_id, but they store free-text q. A nullable actor column alone does not establish population or anonymisation; query text and other logs need an identifiability review.

### Rights and response times

* **Access — Act s. 24(1)–(9):** on proof of identity, a subject may ask whether data is held, for a description, and for the identity/category of third parties with access. The controller must comply promptly and in any event within 30 days (**s. 24(9)**), subject to the other-person safeguards in s. 24(4)–(8).
* **Reg. 35(1)–(4):** access requests use Form 8; acceptable identity evidence includes a national/alien ID, passport/travel document, or driving licence; the controller informs the subject of its decision within seven days and gives reasons for refusal. Because Reg. 35 says seven days while Act s. 24(9) says full compliance within 30 days, build a seven-day decision/identity workflow and a 30-day completion ceiling, and have counsel reconcile the interaction.
* **Correction/deletion — Act s. 16(1)–(5), Reg. 29:** a subject may request correction/deletion of inaccurate, irrelevant, excessive, outdated, incomplete, misleading, unlawfully obtained, or no-longer-authorised-to-retain data. The controller must decide in writing within seven days under **Reg. 29(2)**, comply where satisfied under s. 16(2), give written reasons for rejection, and tell prior recipients of corrections. A complaint may follow after 30 days if not complied with (**Reg. 39(1)–(8)**; the statutory text contains several form cross-references, so use the current PDPO forms).
* **Stop harmful processing — Act s. 25(1)–(5):** written notice may require stopping processing causing or likely to cause unwarranted substantial damage/distress; respond within 14 days, give reasons and (where required) the Authority a copy, with a possible Authority direction within seven days. The original Act text and ULII consolidation say the exclusion is s. 7(2); the current NITA-U Cap. 97 PDF appears to print “s. 3(2)” at s. 25(5), an apparent cross-reference error. Verify this point against the Gazette/counsel before relying on the exception.
* **Direct marketing — Act s. 26(1)–(6):** a subject may require stopping processing for direct marketing; respond within 14 days. “Direct marketing” includes advertising/marketing material directed at an individual. **Reg. 37** lets a subject ask the Office within 14 days to review a decision to continue direct marketing; the Office reviews within 14 days.
* **Automated decisions — Act s. 27(1)–(6), Reg. 38:** for a decision significantly affecting a subject, the subject may require that it not be based solely on automated processing. If solely automated, notify as soon as reasonably practicable and allow a written reconsideration request within 21 days; respond on the steps taken within 21 days. Contract-formation/performance decisions and legally authorised/required decisions are excluded under s. 27(4). Regulation 38 gives a 14-day response/reasons path and a possible Office direction within seven days.
* **Rectification/erasure orders and complaints — Act ss. 28, 31–33:** the Office may order rectify/update/block/erase/destroy inaccurate data and require notice to prior third parties; any person may complain; a subject suffering damage/distress may seek compensation in court.

### Security, processors, and breach notification

* **Act s. 20(1)–(3):** controllers, collectors, and processors must adopt appropriate, reasonable technical and organisational measures against loss, damage, unauthorised destruction/access, and unauthorised processing; identify risks, maintain safeguards, verify them, update them, and follow accepted/security industry practices.
* **Act s. 21(1)–(2):** a controller must not permit a processor to process unless the processor establishes/complies with the Act’s security measures; the controller–processor contract must require confidentiality and necessary security measures.
* **Act s. 22(1)–(2):** an operator/person processing for the controller acts only with the controller’s prior knowledge/authorisation and must keep data confidential; a processor must not disclose unless required by law or in discharging a duty. **Reg. 32** adds that the controller must ensure a processor develops/implements appropriate safeguards and provides a processor complaint route.
* **Breach timing — Act s. 23(1)–(5), Reg. 33(1)–(5):** where a collector, processor, or controller believes personal data was accessed/acquired by an unauthorised person, it must notify the Authority in the prescribed manner **immediately** and state remedial action. Regulation 33 says the notification is made immediately after the breach and uses Form 7; it lists the breach nature, affected data, categories/approximate number of subjects, likely consequences, remedial measures, and DPO/point-of-contact details. The Authority decides whether the data subject must be notified and gives guidance. There is no 72-hour deadline in the Act or Regulations reviewed; do not import a GDPR 72-hour rule.

### Cross-border processing and Groq

* **Act s. 19(a)–(b):** where a Uganda-based controller/processor processes or stores personal data outside Uganda, it must ensure the destination has protection at least equivalent to the Act or obtain the subject’s consent.
* **Reg. 30(1)–(6):** the organisation must demonstrate adequacy to the Office or obtain consent; a transfer from the approved foreign destination to a third country needs the subject’s express consent; consent must account for the data’s nature; the Office is to Gazette adequate countries; if a destination is not listed, the organisation bears the proof burden; non-compliance is an offence with a daily fine up to two currency points or up to three months’ imprisonment (or both).
* The AI call carrying free-user text and 12-message history to Groq is a likely cross-border disclosure/processing issue. Confirm the actual endpoint, storage, retention, support access, sub-processors, and destination countries; identify Groq in the notice/recipient record; use a processor contract with confidentiality/security/no-secondary-purpose terms; and document Act s. 19/Reg. 30 adequacy or consent before sending personal data. The official source set reviewed did not establish that a particular Groq country is on a PDPO adequacy Gazette list.

### Cookies, analytics, marketing, and automated processing

* The Act/Regulations reviewed contain no standalone cookie-consent section. The PDPO classification guidance nevertheless expressly treats “Internet or similar network activity” collected by cookies/similar technologies as a data category, including IP addresses, user logs, browsing/search history, and interactions with a website, application, or advertisement. Apply s. 3, s. 7, s. 12, s. 13, security, retention, and rights duties to identifiable tracking data; state the purposes, providers, retention, and opt-out/control route in the notice.
* For promotional email/SMS/push or personalised advertising, use the direct-marketing rule in **s. 26** and **Reg. 37**. Maintain suppression/opt-out records; a subject’s written notice must be honoured or answered within 14 days. PDPO complaint guidance also lists direct marketing without consent as a complaint example.
* Search ranking, recommendation, fraud checks, or eligibility decisions that significantly affect a person may trigger **s. 27/Reg. 38** if solely automated. Provide notice and a human/reconsideration path where the rule applies; document contract/law exceptions rather than assuming they apply.

## Product-facing drafting checklist

1. Name the Akolet/CampusHomes controller entity and DPO, and make the notice enumerate account/contact, listing, ID/passport, agreement, health/allergy, support/AI prompt/history, search/event, analytics/cookie, and payment/marketing data categories.
2. For every category, state purpose, s. 7 route (consent/contract/legal obligation/etc.), whether mandatory, recipients, cross-border destination, retention/deletion rule, and rights/contact route.
3. Separate consent for optional health/allergy data and any other special data; avoid bundling optional processing into a general acceptance checkbox.
4. Correct the “never store search text” claim or remove the query from product-event storage. Document the actor/query/listing identifiers retained and call the resulting data personal where linkable.
5. Treat Groq as a processor only after confirming its role, contract, destination/transfer safeguards, retention, sub-processors, and deletion behavior. Do not send unnecessary history or identifiers.
6. Build and test rights workflows for Reg. 35’s seven-day access decision, Act s. 24’s 30-day outer limit, Reg. 29’s seven-day correction/deletion decision, and 14-day objection/direct-marketing responses.
7. Implement an immediate PDPO breach notification runbook; keep Form 7 data ready and do not wait for a 72-hour timer.
8. Map purge across app DB, identity provider (including failed/best-effort calls), object storage, events/logs, backups, and vendors; document lawful holds and de-identification where deletion is not required.
9. Confirm PDPO registration, DPO designation, renewal date, registered purposes/categories/recipients/transfers, and any current Gazette exemptions directly with PDPO before launch.
